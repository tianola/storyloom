**Research date / access date for every web source:** 2026-11-09

**Retrieval budget actually used:** 12 WebSearch calls; 8 WebFetch calls

**Method:** Single-agent research; no subagents, downloads, uploads, paid calls, or production-file changes.

## 1. How to read this report

### Source tiers

- **Tier 1 — primary or authoritative:** contemporary records, peer-reviewed research, NASA/NOAA/USGS, National Academies, FERC/NERC, Library of Congress, and official institutional records.
- **Tier 2 — credible secondary or specialist:** observatory/museum interpretation, a preprint not independently checked against its published version, and official system-operator material that is not a primary scientific record.
- **Tier 3 — locator/supplementary:** Wikimedia and aggregators used to locate assets, not to establish scientific or historical narration.

### Evidence labels

- **Verified in retrieved text:** the cited wording was present in a successfully fetched page.
- **Snippet-only:** the wording came from a search-result extract. It is useful but its surrounding context has not been checked.
- **Inference:** a synthesis of cited evidence, not a direct quotation or direct observation.
- **Unknown / not verified:** the available evidence does not justify scripting the claim.

Confidence describes support for a claim, not source prestige. A Tier 1 snippet can still leave a claim at medium or low confidence.

## 2. Executive findings

The strongest documentary spine is unusually well supported: a major auroral episode occurred on 28–29 August 1859; Richard Carrington and Richard Hodgson independently observed a white-light solar flare on 1 September; a second major storm followed on 2–3 September; and telegraph systems were disrupted over a large geographic area. Green and Boardsen reconstruct a CME arrival roughly 17 hours after the flare, but that causal sequence is a modern reconstruction, not something Carrington could observe directly. [S02]

The most defensible description is therefore **“a benchmark extreme solar–terrestrial event”**, not an unqualified “largest storm in history.” The retrieved literature explicitly debates storm intensity and the interpretation of the Bombay magnetometer. Green and Boardsen repeat a published −1760 nT Dst estimate only to argue that local auroral-electrojet and magnetopause currents probably contaminated the measurement. A precise alternative value was not verified within this research budget. [S02][S04][S25]

The telegraph story should be dramatic without becoming fictional. The retrieved evidence supports reports of **sparks, fires, electric shocks, disrupted circuits, and lines unusable for eight hours or more**. It does not verify the frequently repeated Boston transcript, operators running for an extended period on “auroral power,” a named telegraph paper fire, or a global destruction of the telegraph network. Those details require a primary Loomis/newspaper passage before use. [S02][S03][S12]

For the modern act, the evidence supports a chain from an Earth-directed eruption to geomagnetic disturbance, surface geoelectric fields, and interference with long grounded conductors. USGS modelling identifies especially consequential Carrington-scale geoelectric hazards in parts of the eastern and midwestern United States. This is a scenario and a hazard map, not a forecast of a continent-wide collapse. [S09][S10][S11][S12][S14]

The safest engineering payoff is that mitigation already exists as a combination of monitoring, geoelectric mapping, vulnerability assessment, transmission planning, and reliability standards. FERC approved TPL-007-1 in 2016, and later assessment work shows continuing refinement. The retrieved evidence does not support saying that the grid is either fully protected or defenceless. [S12][S13][S17][S18][S19][S20]

### Timing caveat

Green and Boardsen warn that 1859 predates standard time. Their reported times default to local time, may vary by an hour, and are generally not precise beyond 30–60 minutes. They also excluded hundreds of reports lacking usable timing. This is an important antidote to an overconfident minute-by-minute reconstruction. [S02]

## 4. Carrington’s observation and drawing

**Section confidence: High for observing context and archival location; Low for exact flare timings until the original article is checked.**

Carrington did not photograph the flare. His broader observing programme used a **4.5-inch refractor**, projection of the solar image, timed transits to derive heliographic coordinates, lettered markings, measurement, and drawings completed by eye. Bhattacharya et al. quote Carrington’s 11-inch projected-disc practice as intended “to allow for unintentional exaggeration.” [S08] This context supports a visual of projection, measurement, and hand drawing rather than an observer staring unprotected at the Sun.

The Royal Astronomical Society search record explicitly identifies an image of the journal article reporting the sunspots in *MNRAS* volume 20 for 1859. [S01] Wikimedia provides a locator page titled “Carrington Richard drawing of 1859 sunspots.jpeg.” [S29] Together these are strong leads for the drawing, but the production should compare the Wikimedia reproduction against the RAS journal plate before treating cropping, labels, orientation, or contrast as authentic.

Green and Boardsen credit both **Carrington and Hodgson** with the independent white-light observation. [S02] A documentary titled around Carrington can still give Hodgson one sentence. Doing so prevents the misleading implication of a single heroic observer.

### What remains unknown

- The exact Carrington clock time and its conversion to UT were not verified from the original paper.
- The frequently quoted duration of the white-light patches was not verified.
- No exact flare X-ray class can be measured directly from an 1859 visual observation. Any modern equivalent is a reconstruction with assumptions; no supporting extract for a specific class was retrieved.
- Carrington did not directly image a CME travelling toward Earth. The flare–CME–storm chain is inferred from timing and later physical understanding. [S02]

These are evidence gaps, not assertions that every anecdote is false.

## 6. Sun-to-ground mechanism

**Section confidence: High for the broad chain; Medium for the 1859-specific CME reconstruction; Unknown for detailed magnetopause geometry not retrieved here.**

### 6.1 Flare and CME are not synonyms

Carrington and Hodgson observed a **white-light flare**. [S02] NOAA defines a coronal mass ejection as a large expulsion of **plasma and magnetic field from the Sun’s corona** and says CMEs can eject billions of tons of coronal material. [S10] NASA describes a solar storm more broadly as a sudden explosion of particles, energy, magnetic fields, and material, driven by magnetic reconnection in its educational account. [S09]

**Documentary wording:** “The white-light flare was the visible flash. The later magnetic storm is associated with an eruption of solar plasma and magnetic field. In 1859, that connection is reconstructed from the observations and timing.” [S02][S09][S10]

**Do not visualize Carrington’s drawing as a CME.** It is a sunspot/flare observation. A separate, clearly labelled modern diagram is needed for the CME.

### 6.2 Arrival and magnetosphere interaction

NASA states that when a storm is aimed at Earth it can disturb our magnetic field, producing a **geomagnetic storm**, with consequences including radio blackouts, power outages, and aurora. [S09] This supports a simple magnetosphere sequence: Earth-directed material and magnetic field arrive; the near-Earth magnetic environment is disturbed; rapid magnetic variations couple to the ground and ionosphere.

A more detailed animation of magnetopause compression, magnetic reconnection at the magnetopause, southward IMF coupling, or ring-current dynamics needs an additional specialist source. None of those details was exposed in the retrieved NASA passage, so they should not be smuggled into narration as if directly verified by this report.

### 6.3 Geoelectric field and geomagnetically induced current

The peer-reviewed *Annual Review* search extract states: “Magnetic storms induce geoelectric fields at Earth’s surface that can interfere with grounded long-line systems.” [S11] USGS explains that the strength varies with local geology and that sufficiently strong fields can stress or damage high-voltage transformers. [S12]

**Reasoned physical chain for the film:**

1. A disturbed geomagnetic field produces a surface geoelectric field. [S11]
2. The field drives current through available conducting paths, including long grounded technological systems. [S11][S33]
3. In 1859 those systems included telegraph lines; today they include parts of power-transmission networks. [S02][S12]
4. Geology and network configuration change the local hazard; a single global voltage or current is not meaningful. [S12][S14]

Step 2 is an evidence-based synthesis, not a claim that telegraph and transformer circuits are identical. Their responses should be animated separately.

## 7. Modern grid impacts: established events versus scenarios

**Section confidence: High for the 1989 event as a documented warning; Medium for regional Carrington-scale model outputs; Low for exact modern loss forecasts.**

### 7.1 The 1989 Québec benchmark

NOAA’s 2015 account says that on 13 March 1989 Québec went dark and introduces a six-million-person impact with “Within 90 seconds.” [S15] NOAA’s *Storms from the Sun* booklet says Hydro-Québec went from full operating capacity to blackout in **92 seconds**, with six million people affected. [S16] USGS independently describes tripped relays, damaged high-voltage transformers, and a blackout. [S17]

**Safe narration:** “In March 1989, a geomagnetic storm collapsed Hydro-Québec’s grid in roughly ninety seconds; NOAA accounts say six million people lost power.” Do not manufacture false precision from the 90/92-second difference. A nine-hour restoration figure was not verified from an official full-text source in this run and should be checked before use.

### 7.2 Carrington-scale geoelectric modelling

USGS reports a new map based on decades of geomagnetic data and Earth-conductivity measurements. It identifies elevated exposure in the East and Midwest and says some modelled fields could stress or damage transformers. [S12] The 2025 AGU paper’s indexed extract reports likely high-strength geoelectric fields in the US East and Midwest, with values “More than 5.00 V/km” in the snippet. [S14]

These are **model results for a scenario**, not measurements of a modern Carrington event. The exact spatial extent and numerical threshold should be taken from the paper’s figures after full-text review, not from a snippet alone.

### 7.3 Other infrastructure

USGS lists satellite operations, GPS, radio communications, telecommunications, power transmission, and possible flight disruption. [S12] NASA lists radio blackouts, power outages, and aurora. [S09] Keep these as possible effects of severe space weather, not a promise that every service fails simultaneously.

USGS’s “days or weeks” wording belongs to a **worst case**. [S12] It must carry that qualifier on screen and in narration.

### What not to claim

- “Nothing can be done.” Contradicted by monitoring, mapping, assessment, and standards. [S12][S18]
- “The grid is now Carrington-proof.” Not supported; USGS quotes Love saying an intense storm could exceed industry preparedness. [S12]
- “Blocking devices/series capacitors/grid reconfiguration will solve it.” These are plausible engineering topics for further research, but no supporting extract on their effectiveness was retrieved here.
- “A Carrington event is exactly a once-in-100-years event.” USGS quotes a qualitative “100-year-type events” description, while the Royal Academy of Engineering extract says recurrence statistics are poor, though improving. [S12][S24]

## 10. Claims that should NOT be repeated

The following should be excluded from narration unless later primary evidence changes the ruling:

1. Carrington saw a CME or understood the full Sun–Earth causal chain.
2. The entire global telegraph network was destroyed.
3. Telegraph operators were electrocuted or killed; no such evidence was retrieved.
4. A named operator transcript, battery-disconnection exchange, or fire anecdote presented as verified fact.
5. A precise X-class, definitive Dst, exact modern death toll, or unqualified multi-trillion-dollar loss.
6. “Aurora at the equator” without a specific observation and coordinate check.
7. “We are overdue,” “it will happen next solar maximum,” or any deterministic recurrence date.
8. “Every satellite, transformer, computer, and vehicle would fail.”
9. “There is no defence,” or conversely, “existing standards eliminate the danger.”
10. Any equation, animation, or scale implying that one geoelectric-field value applies uniformly across Earth. [S12][S14]

## 12. Recommended narrative formulations

These are evidence-safe options, not a locked script:

- **Opening:** “Before the famous flare, the sky had already erupted into aurora. In late August 1859, one magnetic storm was recorded. A second, even more consequential episode would follow within days.” [S02]
- **Observation:** “On 1 September, Richard Carrington and, independently, Richard Hodgson observed a white-light flare. Carrington’s instrument projected the Sun; the record was made by measurement and hand drawing.” [S02][S08]
- **Transit:** “In Green and Boardsen’s reconstruction, the associated CME reached Earth roughly seventeen hours later.” [S02]
- **Telegraph:** “The next storm drove currents into long telegraph circuits. Systems failed; reports describe sparks, fires, and electric shocks. Some lines were unusable for eight hours or more.” [S02][S12]
- **Modern warning:** “In 1989, Québec offered a modern demonstration: six million people lost power after a grid collapse that unfolded in roughly ninety seconds.” [S15][S16]
- **Present risk:** “A Carrington-scale storm is not a uniform global off-switch. Geology and network design shape where the strongest geoelectric hazard appears.” [S12][S14]
- **Payoff:** “The response is not prediction alone. It is measurement, mapping, vulnerability assessment, planning standards, and engineering targeted to the places most at risk.” [S12][S18][S19]

## 15. Overall confidence and production ruling

- **High confidence:** two principal late-August/early-September storm episodes; Carrington and Hodgson’s 1 September white-light observation; widespread telegraph disturbance; reports of sparks/fires/shocks; the 1989 Québec blackout as a modern warning; existence of monitoring, mapping, planning, and standards. [S02][S03][S12][S15][S16][S17][S18]
- **Medium confidence:** roughly 17-hour CME association; exact geographic extent and duration of low-latitude aurora; regional model values for a Carrington-scale modern storm; the 200,000 km/8-hour telegraph quantification. These are credible study results but require attribution and, for the model values, full-text context. [S02][S12][S14]
- **Low / not narration-ready:** precise flare class, definitive Dst, exact 17.6-hour transit, named telegraph anecdotes, unqualified $1–2 trillion losses, 4–10-year recovery, exact recurrence interval, and any deterministic “overdue” claim.

**Bottom line:** The event does not need exaggeration. The strongest film moves from a hand-drawn flash on the Sun, through induced current in a nineteenth-century wire, to a modern engineering problem that is measured, mapped, and planned for—but not eliminated.
