In a factory in Taiwan, a drop of molten tin is flying through a vacuum at two hundred and fifty kilometres an hour.
It is about thirty micrometres wide, smaller than the width of a human hair.
A laser pulse hits it and squashes it flat, like a pancake.
A moment later, a far stronger pulse hits the pancake and turns it into plasma, about forty times hotter than the surface of the Sun.
For an instant, that plasma glows with a kind of light that barely exists on Earth.
Then the next drop arrives, and the whole thing happens again, fifty thousand times every second.
The drops, the lasers, the tiny artificial stars: all of it exists to draw lines on a computer chip.
Lines only a few dozen atoms wide.
To understand why anyone would build something this extreme, we need one stubborn fact about light.
By the end, every strange part of this machine will make sense, because each one fights that single fact.
A modern chip is a city of tiny switches called transistors, tens of billions of them, wired together in stacked layers.
Nobody builds them one at a time. They are printed all at once, like a photograph.
It starts with a polished disc of silicon, called a wafer.
The wafer is coated with a thin film called photoresist, a material that changes wherever light falls on it.
Above it sits the mask, a plate carrying the pattern for one layer, drawn four times larger than it will end up.
Light shines through the mask, and a set of optics shrinks the image four times and focuses it onto the wafer.
It is a slide projector run backwards: instead of throwing a small picture onto a wall, it squeezes a big picture onto a tiny patch.
Where the light lands, the resist changes. Wash it, etch it, and the pattern is cut into the layer underneath.
Then the wafer is coated again and the next layer is printed on top, dozens of times.
So the size of a transistor comes down to one question: how small a detail can you project with light?
The answer depends on what light is. Light is a wave.
Watch a wave arrive at a gap. If the gap is wide, the wave passes almost straight through.
But if the gap is narrow, close to the size of the wave itself, the wave spreads out on the other side. This spreading is called diffraction.
A chip pattern is nothing but narrow gaps. A row of fine lines on a mask behaves like what physicists call a grating.
Light passing through a grating does not just carry straight on. It splits into separate beams that fan out at an angle.
And here is the key: the finer the lines, the wider that fan opens.
To rebuild the pattern on the wafer, the lens has to catch those angled beams and bring them back together.
If it catches them, the beams overlap, they interfere, and the lines reappear, sharp.
But if the fan is too wide and the side beams miss the lens, only the straight beam gets through.
The wafer then receives a flat, even glow. The pattern is simply gone.
So there is a hard limit: below a certain size, a line cannot be printed at all.
Engineers write that limit as a short formula.
The smallest feature equals a factor called k one, times the wavelength, divided by the numerical aperture.
Read it as three knobs you can turn.
The numerical aperture measures how wide a fan the lens can collect. A bigger opening catches wider beams.
The factor k one is cleverness: tricks in the lighting and the mask that squeeze out a little more.
And the wavelength is the size of the wave itself. Shorter waves fan out less, so they can draw finer lines.
Every chipmaking machine ever built is an attempt to turn one of these three knobs.
For decades, the industry turned the wavelength knob.
Mercury lamps gave light at four hundred and thirty six nanometres, then three hundred and sixty five.
Then came lasers, at two hundred and forty eight nanometres, and then one hundred and ninety three.
And there the wavelength knob got stuck.
Below about one hundred and fifty nanometres, almost every lens material absorbs the light instead of bending it.
So engineers turned the other knobs.
They filled the gap between the lens and the wafer with water, which bends light more than air, so the lens could collect a wider fan.
When even that was not enough, they split each layer into two, three, even four separate exposures, each printing only some of the lines.
It worked, but every extra pass costs time and money, and has to line up perfectly with the others.
To keep shrinking, the industry had to turn the wavelength knob again, and by an enormous amount.
The target was thirteen and a half nanometres, fourteen times shorter. Extreme ultraviolet light.
In nineteen eighty six, a Japanese researcher, Hiroo Kinoshita, showed the first images printed with it.
Turning that experiment into a factory tool took nearly three decades.
Because at thirteen and a half nanometres, almost everything we know about building optics stops working.
Problem number one: nothing glows at this wavelength. Not a lamp, and not an ordinary laser.
To make it, you need matter so hot that its atoms are stripped of many of their electrons. A plasma.
Tin turns out to be ideal: highly charged tin ions give off much of their light in a narrow band around thirteen and a half nanometres.
So the machine makes tin plasma, one droplet at a time.
A generator fires a stream of molten tin droplets, each about thirty micrometres across.
A carbon dioxide laser, built by the German company Trumpf, waits for them.
Its pulses pass through four amplifier stages, until the beam carries tens of kilowatts.
The first, weaker pulse hits the droplet and flattens it into a thin disc.
Why flatten it? A small sphere is a hard target, and most of its tin is hidden inside.
A wide, thin disc spreads that tin out, where the main pulse can reach all of it.
Then the main pulse hits the disc and turns it into plasma at around two hundred thousand degrees.
Fifty thousand times a second, a tiny star is born and dies inside the machine.
And it is brutally inefficient. Only about five percent of the laser energy comes out as useful light.
A large curved mirror, the collector, gathers the light and focuses it toward a single point.
But every explosion sprays tin at tens of kilometres per second, and even one nanometre of tin on the collector would ruin it.
So a constant flow of hydrogen gas sweeps through the chamber, cleaning the tin away before it can settle.
The result is more than five hundred watts of extreme ultraviolet light, steady enough to run a factory around the clock.
Problem number two: this light is swallowed by everything.
Glass absorbs it, so there can be no lenses.
Even air absorbs it, so the entire light path has to sit in a vacuum.
That leaves mirrors. But an ordinary mirror reflects almost none of this light.
So these mirrors are built from layers: about forty pairs of molybdenum and silicon, each pair only about seven nanometres thick.
Every boundary between the two materials reflects only a tiny bit of the light. On its own, that is useless.
But the layers are spaced at half the wavelength, so every one of those tiny reflections leaves in step with all the others.
The wave crests line up, they add together, and dozens of weak echoes become one strong reflection, about seventy percent.
It is the same trick that makes a soap bubble shimmer, tuned to a single colour.
This is also where the strange number comes from.
Silicon starts absorbing strongly just below twelve and a half nanometres, and these mirrors work best just above that edge.
Thirteen and a half is where the mirrors work best, and where tin happens to glow brightest.
These mirrors must also be smooth beyond belief, with bumps of around fifty picometres, smaller than a single atom.
If one of these mirrors were the size of Germany, its biggest bump would be about one millimetre high.
Zeiss makes them, in southern Germany.
Now follow the light through the machine.
From the collector, it bounces off a set of mirrors that shape it into an even, curved slit of light.
Then it hits the mask. And the mask, too, has to be a mirror.
It is the same stack of molybdenum and silicon, with the chip pattern drawn on top in a metal that absorbs the light.
The light strikes it at a slight angle, about six degrees, so the reflected beam can travel on without hitting the incoming one.
Then six more mirrors shrink the pattern four times and focus it onto the wafer.
Count the bounces. There are around ten of them, and each one keeps only about seventy percent.
Seventy percent of seventy percent of seventy percent, again and again.
Of the light that leaves the source, only a few percent ever reaches the wafer.
That is why the source has to be so violent: almost all its light is spent getting there.
Even then, the machine cannot expose a whole chip at once. The light only covers a thin slit.
So the mask and the wafer move in opposite directions, sweeping the pattern through that slit.
Because the image is shrunk four times, the mask has to travel four times faster than the wafer, in perfect step.
The mask stage whips back and forth with accelerations of more than twenty times the pull of gravity.
Then the wafer steps to the next patch, and the dance repeats, around a hundred times per wafer.
Every new layer has to land on the one beneath it to within about a nanometre. A handful of atoms.
A modern machine does all of this for around two hundred wafers every hour.
You might think a shorter wave simply means sharper lines. But there is a catch.
Light arrives in tiny packets of energy called photons, and a shorter wave packs more energy into each one.
An extreme ultraviolet photon carries about fourteen times the energy of the deep ultraviolet photons used before.
So for the same total dose, the wafer receives about fourteen times fewer photons.
And photons arrive at random, like the first raindrops of a shower on a pavement.
At a typical dose, a square of resist ten nanometres on a side catches only around two thousand of them.
Some patches catch a few more, some a few less.
Where too few land, a line comes out thin or broken. Where too many land, two lines can merge into one.
Engineers call these stochastic effects, and they are now one of the hardest limits in chipmaking.
The cure is more photons: a brighter source, or a longer exposure. And every extra photon costs time.
That is why the race for more source power never stops.
With the wavelength fixed, one knob is left to turn: the numerical aperture.
Today's standard machines collect light with a numerical aperture of zero point three three, enough for lines about thirteen nanometres wide.
The newest generation opens that to zero point five five, for lines of about eight nanometres.
Catching a wider fan means bigger mirrors, and steeper angles at the mask.
So these machines shrink the image eight times one way and four times the other, giving up half the exposure field.
The first commercial one was assembled at Intel, and each costs around three hundred and sixty million dollars.
Only one company in the world sells these machines: the Dutch company ASML.
So look back at that flying drop of tin.
The tin, the laser and the plasma exist because nothing else glows at this wavelength.
The vacuum and the layered mirrors exist because everything else swallows it.
The scanning, the speed and the nanometre precision exist because the light can only fill a slit.
And the endless hunger for power exists because each photon carries so much energy that too few arrive.
All of it traces back to that one stubborn fact: light is a wave, and a wave cannot draw a detail much smaller than itself.
This machine is what it looks like when people refuse to accept that.
The next time you pick up your phone, remember: somewhere inside it are lines drawn by a star smaller than a grain of dust.
