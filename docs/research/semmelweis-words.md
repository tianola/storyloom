## 1. Semmelweis's own words

### 1a. Distress and perplexity [PT, S1 Carter ch. 1]
- "Everything was in question; everything seemed inexplicable; everything was doubtful. Only the large number of deaths was an unquestionable reality."
- The disrespect shown by staff to the first clinic "made me so miserable that life seemed worthless" (paraphrase it; context is staff taunting the first-clinic personnel).
- Perplexity: he compares himself to "a drowning person grasping at a straw" when he switched from supine to lateral deliveries "so that everything would be exactly as in the second clinic." It changed nothing.
- He went to Venice on 2 March 1847 hoping the art would revive "my mind and spirits", which the maternity hospital had badly affected. He returned on 20 March 1847.

### 1b. The priest and the bell [PT Sinclair 1909; Carter wording UNVERIFIED]
- "Even to me myself it had a strange effect upon my nerves when I heard the bell hurried past my door." Sinclair 1909 translation, quoted in S5 (pp. 36-37 per that blog). German original, per Waldheim 1905 p. 13 (S12), footnoted to Ätiologie p. 34: "Mir selbst war es unheimlich zu Mute, wenn ich das Glöckchen an meiner Türe vorbeieilen hörte".
- Radiolab reads "The bell was a painful exhortation to me to search for this unknown cause with all of my might" (S6, translator not named). Treat as [POP] until checked.
- Physical facts [SS, via search snippets of Hempel/UCL and S4]: the priest in robes, with an attendant ringing a bell, passed through five wards of the first clinic to reach the sickroom; in the second clinic he had direct access. Semmelweis had the priest stop the bell and use a quiet route; the figures did not change.

### 1c. Women begging [PT via S4, SS via S2]
- S2 cites Semmelweis 1983 (Carter) p. 70: desperate women begged on their knees not to be admitted to the first clinic.
- S4 gives the Carter wording (p. 70) for the patients who "kneeling and wringing their hands, beg to be released in order to seek admission to the second clinic." Safest exact wording. I could not read the full sentence in context.
- Related, from S1: women who delivered "prematurely or on the street almost never became ill". Paraphrase (see "street births" below).

### 1d. Eliminating the causes [partly PT, partly SS]
- From S1: he was convinced the excess was due to "an endemic but as yet unknown cause"; newborns also got childbed fever, so the disease was "misconceived".
- Position: he switched to lateral delivery to copy the second clinic (quote in 1a). No improvement.
- Commission measures: Carter ch. 1 says none of the measures the commissions instituted brought the first clinic's mortality to the second clinic's level. Paraphrase.
- Overcrowding [SS, search snippet of UCL/Hempel, matches Semmelweis 1861]: the second clinic was always more crowded and still had lower mortality. UNVERIFIED wording.
- Miasma/atmospheric-cosmic-telluric influences [SS, same]: both clinics share one roof and one air. UNVERIFIED wording.
- Rough examination by students [SS, same]: a commission at the end of 1846 suggested the first-clinic students, especially the foreign ones, examined more roughly than the student midwives; student numbers were cut from 42 to 20 and mortality did not fall. UNVERIFIED wording.
- Exact Carter wording for these three was not freely available. Carter is on archive.org (borrow): https://archive.org/details/etiologyconcepta0000unse

### 1e. Kolletschka and "cadaverous particles" [PT, S1 Carter ch. 1]
- "On 20 March of the same year, a few hours after returning to Vienna" he resumed work and learned that Kolletschka, "whom I greatly admired", had died.
- "Day and night I was haunted by the image of Kolletschka's disease."
- "Not the wound, but contamination of the wound by the cadaverous particles caused his death."
- "I was compelled to ask whether cadaverous particles had been introduced into the vascular systems" of the women. "I was forced to answer affirmatively."
- Soap does not remove them: "the cadaverous smell that the hands retain". Strong physical detail.
- Case history as he tells it: a student pricked Kolletschka's finger with the autopsy knife (he does not recall which finger); lymphangitis and phlebitis, then pleurisy, pericarditis, peritonitis, meningitis; a metastasis formed in one eye.

### 1f. The chlorine order [PT, S1 Carter ch. 1]
- "To destroy cadaverous matter adhering to hands I used chlorina liquida." The practice began "in the middle of May 1847"; he no longer remembered the day. Students and he were required to wash before examinations. Later he switched to chlorinated lime because of cost.
- Rule: every examiner must wash in chlorinated lime on entering the labour room.
- October 1847: a patient with discharging uterine cancer; the examiners washed with soap only; "of twelve patients then delivering, eleven died." So the rule was extended to washing between patients.
- November 1847: a patient with a carious knee; eleven died in November and eight in December.

### 1g. Mortality (Semmelweis's own tables, Carter S1)
- 1841-46 first clinic: 1,989 deaths of 20,042 births (avg 9.92%); second clinic: 691 of 17,791 (3.38%). 1846: first clinic 459 of 4,010 (11.4%), second clinic 105 of 3,754 (2.7%).
- 1847 first clinic: 176 of 3,490 (5%); second clinic 32 of 3,306 (0.9%). May 1847: 36 of 294 (12.24%). June-Dec 1847: 56 of 1,841 (3.04%); June 6 of 268, July 3 of 250.
- 1848 first clinic: 45 of 3,556 (1.27%); second clinic 43 of 3,219 (1.33%). March and August 1848: no deaths.
- Transfers of the sick to the General Hospital in bad periods made the first clinic's official figures look better than reality (Carter ch. 1).
- S2 (Wikipedia, citing Ataman et al. 2013) gives other monthly figures (18.3% April 1847, 2.2% June, 1.2% July, 1.9% August). Use one source per scene; I recommend Carter's tables.

### 1h. Guilt [PT, S1 Carter ch. 1]
- "only God knows the number of patients who went prematurely to their graves because of me." Followed by: he examined corpses "to an extent equaled by few other obstetricians."
- "the remedy does not lie in suppression": the truth "must be made known to everyone concerned."
- Radiolab (S6) reads a variant, "God only knows the number ... by my fault": use Carter's.

### 1i. Open letters [German originals via S7; English by the Springer authors]
- Letter to Scanzoni, 1861 (Zwei offene Briefe an Dr. J. Spaeth ... und an Hofrath Dr. F. W. Scanzoni, Pest: Emich, pp. 20-21): "erkläre ich Sie vor Gott und der Welt für einen Mörder." English per S7: "I declare you, by God and in presence of the whole world, as a murderer." Conditional: if he keeps teaching the epidemic doctrine without refuting Semmelweis. Same letter: he would immortalise Scanzoni as a "medicinischen Nero".
- Letter to Siebold, 1861, pp. 12-14: "nicht meiner Meinung zu sein, ist gleichbedeutend mit ein Mörder sein." (Not agreeing with me is the same as being a murderer.)
- 1862 Open Letter to All Professors of Obstetrics: popularly quoted "The killing must end, and to bring it to an end, I shall stand guard" and "Whoever dares to promote dangerous doctrines about childbed fever will find in me a resolute opponent" (S8 [POP]). I could not read the original text. Sinclair's version is reported as "In this massacre you, Herr Professor, have participated. The homicide must cease" (search snippet; Radiolab S6 echoes "partner in a massacre"). Verify before use.
- Carter calls the 1862 letters "highly polemical and superlatively offensive" (Carter 1983 p. 57; Carter & Carter 2005 p. 73, via S2). Useful for narration.
- Epilogue of the 1861 book [PT Carter, S1]: "I see a time when only cases of self-infection will occur in the maternity hospitals of the world." Then: even if he does not live to see it, "my death will nevertheless be brightened by the conviction" that the time will come. He also writes that he does not polemicise "because of pugnaciousness", citing "My four years of silence."

### 1j. Döbling / last days
- No first-person words from the asylum found. Carter & Carter 2005 p. 75: "It is impossible to appraise the nature of Semmelweis's disorder." (via S2). Do not invent.

## 2. Contemporaries

- Kolletschka: no documented speech anywhere in the sources.
- Johann Klein: Waldheim (S12, p. 22) quotes Kussmaul's account of Klein saying "Sie haben recht!"; context unverified. Klein called the publication of the clinic protocols a "Denunziation" (Waldheim p. 48, via fetch summary). Carter & Carter: Klein probably mistrusted Semmelweis (S2). Klein's non-renewal of the assistantship ended on 20 March 1849.
- Hebra: published Semmelweis's findings in the Zeitschrift der k.k. Gesellschaft der Ärzte zu Wien, Dec 1847 (vol 4, pp. 242-44) and 1848/49 (vol 5, pp. 64-65), and compared them to Jenner's smallpox vaccination (S2, Waldheim p. 38). Waldheim p. 23 quotes the notice's opening: "Die Redaktion dieser Zeitschrift fühlt sich verpflichtet". The 1865 asylum story involving Hebra is disputed; keep him out of it.
- Skoda: supporter. Waldheim p. 21 quotes Skoda (1849) that colleagues "zweifelten keinen Augenblick, daß sich die Ansicht als richtig erproben werde". Skoda and Rokitansky supported extending his post (S2). Skoda supervised the commission that was blocked "by higher authorities" (Carter ch. 1).
- Rokitansky: supported Semmelweis until he left Vienna in 1850; headed pathological anatomy since 1828 (Carter footnote, S1).
- Virchow: listed among those who rejected the doctrine (Hauzman 2006 via S2). No verified Virchow quote found. Do not script him speaking.
- Scanzoni: took personal offence and never forgave him (S11, from Hauzman 2006); ardent critic.
- Carl Braun: assistant from 20 March 1849 (Carter ch. 1). Carter's footnote: consistently hostile, careless with prophylaxis, rejected the etiology.
- Johann Chiari: no documented quote found in any readable source.
- August Breisky: called the book "the Koran of puerperal theology" (Breisky 1861, via S2). Good critic line.
- Midwives and patients: only as Semmelweis describes them (the begging women, the bell).

## 3. Scene detail

- Hospital: Allgemeines Krankenhaus, Vienna. Maternity hospital opened 16 August 1784 (Carter ch. 1). Two clinics by 1833 (S10). All male students to the first clinic, all female (midwife) students to the second, by decrees of 10, 17 and 27 October 1840 (Carter ch. 1).
- Admission rota (Carter ch. 1): the first clinic admitted from Monday 4 p.m. for 24 hours, then the second from Tuesday 4 p.m., alternating; on Friday 4 p.m. the first clinic took 48 hours through Sunday 4 p.m. The first clinic therefore admitted four days a week, the second three: 52 more admission days a year.
- Registers: reports were prepared in triplicate; one copy stayed in the clinic, one went to the government (Carter ch. 1).
- Old figures: 1784-1823 under 1% died; 1822: 26 of 3,066 (0.84%) vs 1841: 237 of 3,036 (7.7%); 1842: 518 of 3,287 (15.8%) in the first clinic.
- Semmelweis's position: assistant in Klein's first clinic from 1 July 1846 (S2). His predecessor was Dr. Breit, who left for a professorship in Tübingen at the end of February 1847 (Carter ch. 1). The sequence of Breit's tenure and Semmelweis's two stints is unclear in the excerpt; check before dramatising.
- Kolletschka: born 24 July 1803; died 13 March 1847 aged 43; professor of forensic medicine (S3, citing Semmelweis 1861 pp. 87-88). Semmelweis was in Venice.
- Handwashing: chlorina liquida at first, then chlorinated lime for cost, from mid-May 1847. Entering the labour room: wash in chlorinated lime. From October 1847: wash between patients.
- Street births: Gassengeburten. Women who delivered prematurely or on the street almost never became ill (Carter ch. 1). They were nonetheless often admitted afterwards; keep to Carter's statement.
- Dismissal: term ended 20 March 1849; docent only on 10 October 1850 (Carter p. 105 via S2); then to Pest.
- Pest: Szent Rókus Hospital from 20 May 1851; 8 deaths in 933 births (0.85%) 1851-55 (Carter pp. 106-108 via S2).
- Book 1861: Die Ätiologie, der Begriff und die Prophylaxis des Kindbettfiebers. Open letters 1861 (Spaeth, Scanzoni, Siebold) and 1862 (all professors).
- Death: 13 August 1865, aged 47. Certificate dated 19 August 1865, held by the Semmelweis Museum, Budapest (S9). Autopsy: pyemia from a gangrenous wound on the right hand (S9).

## 4. Contested points, phrase with care

- Cause of death: certificate and autopsy say pyemia (blood poisoning) from an infected wound on the right hand. Popular accounts say guards beat him; the sources I read say "reportedly" or "likely" (S6: historians debate). Safe line: "He died two weeks after being committed, of blood poisoning from an infected wound; accounts say he was beaten."
- Which asylum: S9 and S2 place his death in the Lazarettgasse asylum in Vienna; S8 and many popular sources say Oberdöbling. Treat "Döbling" as the popular version, flag it, or say "an asylum in Vienna".
- Hebra "luring" him: repeated in S2; the role of Hebra and others is debated. Say "he was taken there".
- Ignored vs partly accepted: both. Hebra and Skoda announced the finding in Dec 1847; Rokitansky backed him; Michaelis (Kiel) and Lange (Heidelberg) acknowledged it, and Routh promoted it in Britain (S7, S2). Chlorine washing was partly kept in Vienna after he left (Maisel via S7). Scanzoni, Braun, Virchow, Siebold, Späth rejected it. He published late (1861) and offensively.
- Nature of the illness: unresolved (Carter & Carter p. 75).
- By 1861 he also accepted putrid matter from living patients (ichor), not only cadavers (Carter ch. 1). Do not say he blamed only corpses.
- He did not know of germs. His term is decomposing animal-organic matter ("cadaverous particles"). Do not have him say "bacteria".
- Dates: some popular sources say 1846 for the discovery; it is March to May 1847.

## 5. Misattributed or doubtful lines to avoid
- "Doctors are gentlemen, and gentlemen's hands are clean": often attached to him; I found it in none of the sources read. Avoid.
- "Savior of mothers": later epithet (S8).
- "Semmelweis reflex": a modern coinage, not a historical quote.
- Radiolab's "partner in a massacre" is a spoken paraphrase, not a verified translation.
- Quote websites vary the English of the 1862 "killing must end" line. Cite S7/Sinclair or the original German, not a quote site.
- Carter 1983 is in copyright: keep to short quotes. Sinclair 1909 English is public domain but archaic.
